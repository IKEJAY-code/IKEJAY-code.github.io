A paper usually preserves the final path. Research also contains questions that did not work, experiments that failed, and conversations that changed a hypothesis. Some of those are worth keeping.

## Start with a specific question

Instead of only writing “read three papers today,” add one more sentence: **What question was I trying to answer?**

The question can be small. Why does a change help in one setting but not another? What evidence is still missing from an explanation that sounds reasonable?

> The point of a notebook is not to make every day look productive. It is to make changes in judgment traceable.

## Separate observations from explanations

An experiment produces observations. A researcher proposes explanations. Keeping those apart makes a note easier to revisit.

| What to record | A useful prompt |
| --- | --- |
| Question | What judgment am I testing? |
| Conditions | What data, configuration, and scope did I use? |
| Observation | What actually happened? |
| Explanation | What might explain it, and what are the alternatives? |
| Next step | Which experiment would best distinguish those explanations? |

This table is a notebook structure, not an experimental result.

## Leave room for uncertainty

Not every note needs a polished conclusion. “I do not know yet” can be an accurate record. Leaving room for uncertainty makes it possible to return with a better explanation.

This notebook is meant to grow slowly. The process, as well as the finished paper, is worth remembering.
